## 4. Lower-confidence-bound ranking with few observations

**Found a peer-reviewed conference example, but with an important methodological caveat.**

**Reference:** Mo Zhou, Alison Cliff, Sanjay Krishnan, Brandie Nonnecke, Camille Crittenden, Kanji Uchino, and Ken Goldberg (2015). “M-CAFE 1.0: Motivating and Prioritizing Ongoing Student Feedback During MOOCs and Large on-Campus Courses using Collaborative Filtering.” *Proceedings of the 16th Annual Conference on Information Technology Education* (SIGITE ’15), 153–158.  
**DOI:** `10.1145/2808006.2808020`. Authors and conference/DOI are verified in the authors’ manuscript; pagination is corroborated by a published literature survey. ([goldberg.berkeley.edu](https://goldberg.berkeley.edu/pubs/Zhou-M-CAFE-1.0-SIGITE-2015-v29.pdf))

**Verbatim quote — §4.2.2, manuscript p. 4:**
> “Instead, we compute the Wilson score for each idea using the lower bound of the binomial proportion confidence interval.”

The preceding discussion explicitly identifies small numbers of peer ratings as a reason not to rank by mean or median alone. **However**, the subsequent implementation describes a ten-point rating scale and ranking by a mean-minus-standard-error expression. That is not the usual Wilson binomial lower bound. Therefore, this is evidence of published **lower-bound ranking practice**, but it is not a clean methodological validation of Wilson ranking for binary reviews. ([goldberg.berkeley.edu](https://goldberg.berkeley.edu/pubs/Zhou-M-CAFE-1.0-SIGITE-2015-v29.pdf))

**NOT FOUND:** In this search, a clean peer-reviewed or textbook source validating the specific rule “use the Wilson lower bound to quality-gate binary review items with few observations.” The interval-estimation papers in items 1–3 should not be represented as ranking-policy studies.

## 5. The n ≈ 30 guideline

**Verified textbook example:** Alexander Holmes, Barbara Illowsky, and Susan Dean (2017). *Introductory Business Statistics*. OpenStax, §7.1, “The Central Limit Theorem for Sample Means.” This is a textbook, not a journal/conference; volume is not applicable, and the verified HTML section has no page numbering.  
**URL:** https://openstax.org/books/introductory-business-statistics/pages/7-1-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sample-means  
The textbook page verifies authors, title, and publication date. ([openstax.org](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-business-statistics/pages/7-1-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sample-means?modal=KS))

**Verbatim quote — §7.1:**
> “which has been shown to be only 30 observations or more.”

**Qualification:** This verifies that the textbook teaches the 30-observation convention; its surrounding wording is overly categorical. The CLT concerns convergence as sample size increases, not a universal switch at 30. OpenStax’s statistics text states that asymptotic formulation, and Brown et al. warn that common textbook prescriptions for Wald-interval safety can be misleading. Thus **n = 30 is a convention, not a theorem guaranteeing adequate normal approximation or stable proportion estimates**. ([openstax.org](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/7-1-the-central-limit-theorem-for-sample-means-averages))

**NOT FOUND:** An authoritative justification that **30 observations universally suffice for stable proportion estimates**, or that Wilson intervals require at least 30 observations. Indeed, Agresti and Coull explicitly report favorable score-interval coverage for very small samples. ([tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00031305.1998.10480550?utm_source=openai))

## 6. Independence, review fraud, and “one vote per author”

### Luca and Zervas (2016)

**Verified reference:** Michael Luca and Georgios Zervas (2016). “Fake It Till You Make It: Reputation, Competition, and Yelp Review Fraud.” *Management Science*, **62**(12), 3412–3427.  
**DOI:** `10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304`. ([pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304?utm_source=openai))

**Verbatim quote — abstract:**
> “First, roughly 16% of restaurant reviews on Yelp are filtered.”

**Important correction:** The authors treat reviews flagged by Yelp’s filtering algorithm as a **proxy** for fraud. “16% were filtered” is verified; “16% were proven fake” is not. This is also a finding from their study, not a verified current platform-wide fraud rate. ([pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304?utm_source=openai))

### Mayzlin, Dover, and Chevalier (2014)

**Verified reference:** Dina Mayzlin, Yaniv Dover, and Judith Chevalier (2014). “Promotional Reviews: An Empirical Investigation of Online Review Manipulation.” *American Economic Review*, **104**(8), 2421–2455.  
**DOI:** `10.1257/aer.104.8.2421`. ([moodle.epfl.ch](https://moodle.epfl.ch/pluginfile.php/2835201/mod_resource/content/1/mayzlin14promotional.pdf?utm_source=openai))

**Verbatim quote — abstract, p. 2421:**
> “Firms’ incentives to manufacture biased user reviews impede review usefulness.”

The study compares a customer-restricted reviewing platform with one allowing anyone to post and finds patterns consistent with promotional manipulation. That supports concern about review authenticity and participation restrictions, not specifically an experimentally validated one-author-one-vote policy. ([moodle.epfl.ch](https://moodle.epfl.ch/pluginfile.php/2835201/mod_resource/content/1/mayzlin14promotional.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### Lorenz et al. (2011): peer-reviewed support for the independence concern

**Verified reference:** Jan Lorenz, Heiko Rauhut, Frank Schweitzer, and Dirk Helbing (2011). “How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, **108**(22), 9020–9025.  
**DOI:** `10.1073/pnas.1008636108`. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3107299/?utm_source=openai))

**Verbatim quote — abstract:**
> “even mild social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect in simple estimation tasks.”

This provides peer-reviewed experimental support for the Surowiecki-type concern about preserving independent judgments and diversity. It supports **“social influence can undermine aggregation,”** not the stronger universal claim that every beneficial aggregation mathematically requires strict independence. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3107299/))

**NOT FOUND:** Direct evidence in these sources that **“one vote per author” specifically reduces review spam**, or that this rule ensures independent observations. Treat that rule as a proposed design safeguard rather than an established conclusion of Luca–Zervas, Mayzlin–Dover–Chevalier, or Lorenz et al.

**Overall:** The interval references strongly support Wilson/score intervals for small-sample proportion inference. The evidence for lower-bound ranking is more limited and the located example has an implementation inconsistency. The searched sources do not establish a universal 30-observation gate or directly validate one-vote-per-author spam prevention.
